# Logos in Pencraft

A logo stands for one name. It may be three shapes or a full coat of arms with a motto; what makes it a logo
is that it carries one idea, keeps to the rules of one style, is built exactly, and has a form that still
works at 16 pixels. There are two ways to fail. The first is clip art: the subject's most obvious picture
put together from rectangles, a shield and a ribbon added because logos have them, the name in a typewriter
face. The second is timidity: answering every brief with the same flat two-shape mark, when the brief asked
for a badge, a crest, a mascot or something rich. Choose the style the brief calls for and then build it
fully.

Everything in "Designing well" about pages (a ground, a display face, a first view, worlds dealt for the
conversation, two looks as the ceiling) is for pages. For a logo this skill replaces it. The user's own words
come first: a named kind, style, colour, letter or picture is the brief.

Part of this skill is firm and part is a starting point. Firm, for every logo: the order of work below,
one idea, exact construction, the small version and the proofs. Only the user's own words overrule
these. A starting point: what the logo looks like. The worked marks in the reference files show
construction, not a look to reproduce, and the styles are a vocabulary to choose from: a mark that
fits the brief and no listed style is as welcome as one that fits a style.

The order of work: the brief, the style, many ideas, three rough marks, one chosen, built exactly in the
style's own order, type, colour, the small version, the proofs.

The files of this skill:
- `references/styles.md`: every rendering style with its rules, numbers and tells, and how to choose one.
  Read the entry of the style you choose before you draw.
- `references/construction.md`: the numbers every mark is built from (points on a circle, arcs, tangents,
  rotation, cut-outs), with simple worked marks. Read it before the rough marks.
- `references/detail.md`: how a detailed logo is built (the order of passes, line and tone hierarchy, text
  on a circle, rays, stars, wreaths, scrolls, keylines, hatching, organic curves, depth), with worked logos.
  Read it for any style marked detailed.
- `references/wordmarks.md`: choosing the face, spacing, and the sizes and gaps of a lockup.

## 1. The brief, in four lines
Write for yourself, before any layer:
1. The name exactly as it will be set, and what it names (a bakery, a family, a tool for surveyors).
2. Two or three things that are true of this one and not of its neighbours (where it is, how it works, who it
   is for, what its name literally means). Not adjectives like modern, trusted or dynamic.
3. The kind. The user's word decides it; without one, a mark beside a wordmark.
   - Wordmark or logotype: the name alone, set and adjusted.
   - Lettermark or monogram: one to three initials built into one figure.
   - Pictorial or abstract mark with a wordmark beside or under it: the usual "logo".
   - Mascot: a character, usually its head, with the name.
   - Emblem (badge, roundel, seal, crest, coat of arms): picture and name locked into one outline.
   - App icon or favicon: the mark alone on a filled rounded square.
   A logo that also has to work as an app icon is a mark beside a wordmark, plus the icon as a second
   top-level frame.
4. The rendering style and how much detail (section 2).

## 2. Choose the style
`references/styles.md` lists the styles and a table for choosing. The user's words decide: "vintage",
"heritage", "a crest", "mascot", "3D", "glossy", "luxury", "hand-drawn", "detailed", "rich" each name a style
or a level of detail, and so does "minimal" or "simple". Without such a word, choose by the tone of the brief
and where the logo lives, not by habit: a brewery, a football club and a family arms are poorly served by a
two-shape flat mark, and a developer tool is poorly served by a crest. Say in your reply which style you
chose and name one other that would suit.

How much detail is part of the choice:
- Simple (flat geometric, monoline, negative space, mon, luxury, playful): one to three path layers, about a
  dozen shapes at most, one or two colours. The mark is its own small version.
- Detailed (badge, crest, seal, engraving, mascot, dimensional, art deco, psychedelic): as many shapes as the
  style needs, in a hierarchy of three line weights and two or three tones per colour, built in passes. It
  is delivered with a small version (section 9).
When the user asks for more detail in a logo that is there, keep its idea and silhouette and add the next
passes of its style (lines, tone, type, ornament the style owns), not random parts.

A logo keeps to one style. Rules that belong to one (no gradients in a flat mark; one light for every
gradient in a dimensional one; tinctures in heraldry) are in the style's entry.

## 3. Find the idea
Whatever the style, a logo carries one idea you can say in one short sentence: "a K with a V pressed into
its shoulder", "a heron whose neck is the river's bend", "a loaf scored with the baker's initial". In a detailed logo the idea
is the main picture or charge; everything else frames it. Collect before you choose. Write at least eight
candidates from different sources:
- The name's own letters: an initial with one part changed, two initials sharing a stem, a shape hidden in a
  letter's counter.
- What the name literally says or sounds like.
- What the thing does, as a verb (folds, joins, measures, carries).
- Where it is from: one feature of the place only locals would name, not the postcard view.
- A tool, a notation, an animal or a detail of the trade.
- Two of the above fused so that they share a contour, or one sitting in the empty space of the other.

Strike the first picture anyone would draw for the subject, and the stock symbols that stand for nothing:
globe, lightbulb, gear, rocket, leaf, swoosh, orbit rings, puzzle piece, handshake, people in a circle, a
castle or skyline for a city, a row of waves for the sea, an anchor for a port, a cup for coffee, a house
for property. One may survive fused with a second idea that makes it this client's, or, in a badge or crest,
as a charge drawn with a character of its own. A plain initial is not an idea: a letter counts when one part
of it has become something that belongs to this client. A mark a competitor could use unchanged is not
decided yet.

Keep the three candidates that differ most in kind. When the kind is fixed as a monogram, they differ in how
the letters meet; for an emblem, they are three charges in the same outline.

## 4. Three rough marks, then choose
Draw the three candidates side by side in a group named "Logo roughs": each within a box of 240 (an emblem
may be taller), 80 of empty space between the boxes, black (`#111111`), no colour, no type, one to three
`path` layers each. For a detailed style the rough is the skeleton and the silhouette: the outline, its
zones, and the main picture as a solid shape, drawn small here and built anew at 480 once chosen. Take one `screenshot` of the group at `scale: 1`; with it
Pencraft sends a small proof, the row 24 pixels high and enlarged pixel by pixel (without one, look again at
`scale: 0.1`). Choose in this order:
1. Which says its idea without a caption, and is the idea this client's?
2. In the small proof, which is still one clear shape, and which is a blot?
3. Which has a silhouette of its own?
4. Which could someone draw from memory after one look?
The best idea decides, not the tidiest shape. If the best idea blots, draw it again with fewer and thicker
parts. If none passes question 1, go back to the list. Build the final logo in a group of its own, then
delete the whole "Logo roughs" group, the chosen rough with it.

## 5. Build it exactly
Every style rests on the same construction (`references/construction.md`):
- One box, one module. A `viewBox` of `[0, 0, 240, 240]` with a module of 20 for a simple mark; 480 with a
  module of 20 for a detailed one. Straight edges lie on multiples of half a module. A coordinate is on the
  grid or calculated (a point on a circle, a rotated copy), never chosen by feel.
- Curves are arcs of circles with radii in half modules, meeting edges where the tangent agrees. Organic
  shapes are cubic curves with points at the extremes and level or upright handles (`references/detail.md`).
- A small set of angles (0, 45, 90, or 0, 30, 60, 90). Where a line is tangent to two circles, the angle
  wins and the centres are calculated.
- Symmetry is exact: mirrored about the centre line (x becomes W - x), or turned about the centre.
  Repeated parts are the same path moved or rotated by calculation.
- Holes are holes: a second subpath with `"fillRule": "evenodd"`, so the ground shows through. Text is the
  one thing that cannot be cut out; on a filled band it is set in a `logo-paper` colour.
- What lies inside a shape is cut to it by a mask: the shape with `"mask": true`, then what it holds, in one
  group (`references/detail.md`). Never trace a shape's edge by hand to fake a cut.
- The layer fits the mark: `viewBox`, `width` and `height` of its own size; a mark of lines needs half its
  `strokeWidth` of room inside the `viewBox`.
- Gaps of one kind are equal everywhere, and nothing collides: no line through another shape's edge, no
  band cutting off a point, a margin between a charge and its outline.

Then the style's own rules decide weights, tones and effects:
- A simple mark: one weight (or two, one twice the other); filled shapes or lines, not both on one shape;
  no part, gap or line under a twelfth of its width; flat. In a filled letter the one cut that is the idea
  is the exception.
- A detailed logo: built in the passes of `references/detail.md` (skeleton, silhouette, big shapes, lines,
  tone, type, fine detail), with three line weights written down first, two or three tones per colour, one
  light, one centre line, and a resting place. Look at it after every pass and fix that pass before the
  next. Its fine parts may be small, because the small version (section 9) carries the small sizes.
- Gradients, shadows and highlights belong only to the dimensional and app icon styles (and chrome in
  retro), and there they all obey one light from the top.

## 6. Type, and how mark and name sit together
The name is half the logo. `references/wordmarks.md` has the face by the mark's shapes, the spacing by case
and weight, and the numbers of a lockup; each style's entry says what type it takes.
- Choose the face from the installed ones for its shapes, answering the mark and the style. Never the face
  you would have used anyway, and never a typewriter or slab face only to say "tradition".
- One family for the name, one weight, all capitals or written as the name is. A name over two lines may
  have them in two sizes. A detailed emblem may take a second family for one contrasting word, never a third.
- Horizontal lockup: capitals a third to a half as tall as the mark, the gap about the capitals' height, the
  capitals (or the x-height of a mixed-case name) centred on the mark by the eye.
- Stacked lockup: the name centred under the mark, between as wide as the mark and one and a half times as
  wide; if that makes the capitals smaller than a sixth of the mark, go horizontal or to two lines.
- Text round a rim: a `text` layer with `"onCircle": "top"` or `"bottom"` (`references/detail.md` has the
  radii that centre it in a ring). Text on a straight band or scroll: capitals about half the band's height.
  Text on a waving scroll, an arch or a slope: `"onPath"` with the scroll's own middle line. Never fake
  curved text with rotated letters.

## 7. An emblem: badge, roundel, seal, crest, coat of arms
The outline was asked for, so it is part of the logo. The style entries in `references/styles.md` give each
its proportions; what they share:
- The outline is constructed: a circle, a ring, a shield (pointed from two arcs, or flat-topped with a round
  base), a lozenge. Choose the one the charge fills best.
- One main picture or charge, large: as wide as its field allows with a margin of about the heaviest line,
  on the field's visual middle (in a shield, above the middle of its box). No large empty area under it. A
  picture that is not symmetric (an animal in profile) is placed by its mass, not its box; in heraldry it
  faces the viewer's left, elsewhere the way the name reads or toward the centre.
- An emblem owns its field: a disc or shield in `logo-paper` under the picture is part of it, so
  paper-coloured cuts in the picture read on any ground.
- Everything else serves it and sits on the same centre line: the rim text, a second line, separators, a
  scroll, rays. Add a part because the style has a place for it and the brief has something to put there (a
  place, a year, a trade, a motto), not to fill a gap. An empty gap is better than "EST." with nothing true
  after it.
- A simple emblem is one filled outline with the charge cut out of it and the name under it or on a band. A
  detailed one has a ring with text, an inner rule, a picture with its own lines and tones, and perhaps a
  scroll. Both are right; the brief decides.
- A full coat of arms follows heraldry: the parts in their order, tinctures that contrast (never colour on
  colour or metal on metal), stylised bold charges, exact mirror symmetry of mantling and supporters.

## 8. An app icon or a favicon
The mark alone on a filled square frame (1024 with `cornerRadius` 230 for an app icon; 64 with
`cornerRadius` 14 for a favicon), named like "Tidewell app icon". The mark takes about 60% of the tile and is
centred by the eye. Flat: one colour on one colour. Dimensional: a gradient tile lit from the top, one bold
foreground shape with a gradient and one soft shadow, no thin lines, seen straight on (the worked icon in
`references/detail.md`). White is paper and does not count as a colour.

## 9. The small version
A simple mark is its own small version. A detailed logo is delivered with one, as a second top-level group
named like "Holm logo, small": not the big one shrunk but redrawn for 16 to 64 pixels. It keeps the idea
and the silhouette and drops the rest: no rim text, no year, no fine lines, no tone; outlines become solid
shapes, enclosed gaps are opened, every part is at least a twelfth of its width (a twenty-fourth for what
is thin by nature: a leg, a rope, a whisker). Where it needs a ground to be seen (white parts, a mascot
without its contour), it stands on a filled disc or ring in the ink colour. Usually it is the main
charge or the mascot's head, alone or in the outer ring, without the name. Start from the main picture's
own construction and redraw it 240 across with thicker parts, and set it beside the full logo with 80
between.

## 10. Colour
Decide the shapes in black first. Then set the colours as variables (`logo-ink`, `logo-paper`,
`logo-accent`, and shadow tones named after their colour, like `logo-accent-shade`) and use only those. How
many is the style's rule: one or two for a simple mark; two to four flat colours for a badge; a base and a
shadow for each of two or three colours in a mascot; neighbouring hues for a gradient. Choose them from the
subject's own world and not from what every competitor has. A logo must still work in one ink: check that no
two parts that touch are told apart by colour alone, or give them a line or a gap.

## 11. Proofs before you answer
Take these pictures and judge each as a stranger's work; fix what fails and look once more.
1. Small: `screenshot` of the logo at `scale: 1`; the small proof comes with it (the layers 24 pixels on
   their shorter side, enlarged). For a simple mark, and for the small version of a detailed logo: is it one
   clear shape, has nothing filled in or vanished? A detailed logo itself only has to keep its silhouette and
   main picture there.
2. Large: `scale: 2` (and 3 on dense parts). Do curves meet edges without a kink, are both sides mirror
   images, are lines of one tier one weight, are gaps equal, does all shading come from one light, does rim
   text stand upright on its arc and end symmetrically, do capitals sit centred where they should?
3. Hierarchy (a detailed logo): squint. Is there one thing read first, and a place to rest? Or is everything
   equally busy?
4. One ink: would it still work all black?
5. The style: go through the tells of its entry in `references/styles.md`.
6. Whose is it: does the mark look like a known company's? Then change it.
7. The sentence: say the idea in one sentence.
The review that comes with a screenshot counts what it can in a logo (shapes, line weights, colours, lights
that disagree, lines too thin for the small sizes when no small version is there): put right what it names.

## 12. What is delivered
One group, or one frame without a fill, named for the brand and what it is ("Holm logo", "Vorberg crest":
Pencraft's review reads a logo by that name), holding the mark (a path or a group "Mark") and the text layers.
For a detailed logo also its small version beside it. Nothing behind them, no board, no caption, no mock-up;
only an app icon has a filled tile. Do not add one-colour or reversed versions unless asked; offer them. In
your reply: the idea in one sentence, the style and why, the face and the colours, in plain words.

## What gives a generated logo away, in any style
- The subject's obvious picture assembled from rectangles and triangles.
- A container, ribbon, stars or "EST." added because logos have them, not because the style and the brief
  called for them.
- Parts that collide or share no centre line; symmetry that is nearly exact.
- Line weights and gaps that vary without a system; a thick outline and a hairline with nothing between.
- Shading from several lights; grey shadows; a gradient in an otherwise flat mark.
- Waves, flames, leaves or animals drawn as wobbly freehand curves.
- Rim text that collides, runs off centre or stands on its head.
- Detail spread evenly over everything, so nothing is read first.
- The face you used last time; an untouched script font; a typewriter face for tradition.
- The same flat two-shape mark for every brief.
